  1. Venture Description

  Every family in India keeps a folder. Prescriptions, bills, lab reports,
  discharge summaries, all of it stuffed into one plastic bag. Information
  goes in, almost nothing useful comes out. And the person handling it is
  usually not the patient, it is the son or daughter managing a parent's
  treatment, often from another city.

  Right now people cope by carrying the whole pile to every appointment
  and hoping the doctor flips to the right page. Some click photos on
  WhatsApp and lose them in the chat. Some maintain a diary. At claim time
  they run around collecting bills they cannot find. The doctor gets sixty
  pages and about two minutes.

  Parchi lets you photograph that folder. It files every document, answers
  questions about it in plain language, and shows the exact patch of the
  page each answer was read from.
  (139 words)

  2. Market Analysis

  Our customer is the caregiver, not the patient. Typically an adult child
  between 25 and 45, working, urban, managing a parent through diabetes,
  thyroid, cardiac or cancer follow ups. They are the ones actually holding
  the phone and doing the organising, so designing for the patient misses
  the operator.

  Second segment is patients under long term treatment who see multiple
  specialists and carry their own history around.

  We are starting India first, tier 1 and tier 2 cities, English and
  Marathi to begin with. These families already spend on diagnostics and
  consultations, so paying for health is not new to them. They are not
  paying for organisation yet because nobody has offered it properly.

  Doctors and insurers benefit from the same record, but they are not the
  first customer. Those surfaces only work once documents are already in
  the system.
  (142 words)

  3. Competitor Analysis

  Direct: Practo and Tata 1mg store records, but mostly what passes through
  their own platform, so a report from a neighbourhood lab still does not
  land there. Hospital apps like Apollo and Max keep only their own visits.
  ABDM and ABHA are building the rails for exchange, which we support, but
  they do not solve reading a decade of old paper.

  Indirect, and honestly the biggest: WhatsApp, Google Drive, DigiLocker
  and the physical folder itself. That is what families actually use today.

  General AI tools can read one report, but they lose reading order,
  flatten lab tables so a value loses its unit and reference range, and
  sometimes return numbers that are not on the page at all. For medical
  records that is worse than useless.

  Nobody we found ties each extracted value back to the exact region of
  the page it came from.
  (148 words)

  4. Revenue Model

  A paid subscription from the family, and nothing else. We are looking at
  a family plan of a few hundred rupees a month covering multiple members,
  with a free tier limited by pages rather than by features.

  This is deliberate, not a pricing preference. Health records get brokered
  wherever there is a hole in the revenue. If an insurer or a pharma
  company is paying, the family quietly stops being the customer. So we
  keep exactly one customer, the person holding the folder.

  Costs are driven by document processing, storage and model calls.
  Processing is billed per page, so a 200 page folder is where the money
  goes. We are building a router that sends most pages through cheap
  self hosted extraction and only the difficult ones through paid APIs.

  Later, an extraction API for other health apps, and claim tooling for
  TPAs. Both only if the core proves itself.
  (147 words)

  5. Unique Selling Proposition

  One line: every number Parchi shows can be clicked back to the ink on the
  page it came from.

  The rule underneath it is that whatever produces a value is not allowed
  to also certify where it came from. Coordinates come only from a text
  detector. Values may come from a model. A value is stored only if its
  characters are found in detected ink inside that region, and anything
  that cannot be anchored is discarded instead of shown. Most tools cannot
  do this because they output plain text with no coordinates at all.

  Second, we never interpret. We will explain what a test measures. We will
  not tell you what your result means. That keeps us out of medical device
  territory and keeps the trust intact.

  Third, deleting a record destroys its key, so backups hold ciphertext
  nobody can open.

  No patents filed yet.
  (146 words)

  The three findings that matter

  1. You are defending the wrong beachhead.

  Your engineering effort is aimed at extraction correctness. But fewer than 5%
  of India's 900M+ smartphone users have ever opened a health record digitally,
  and the PHR literature is consistent that this is a design failure, not a tech
  failure. The likeliest way Parchi dies is that nobody photographs the folder
  — not that a haemoglobin value comes back wrong.

  Zero users interviewed is the most fixable gap in the project. Not a survey.
  Watch one caregiver try it.

  2. Missing requirement: a death clause.

  23andMe filed Chapter 11 in March 2025. 15 million people's genomes became a
  saleable estate asset — and the mechanism wasn't malice. The US Bankruptcy
  Code looks at the privacy policy to decide whether personal data can be sold,
  so it transfers without normal opt-out rights as long as the buyer keeps the
  same terms.

  Your crypto-shredding defends against a database dump. It does not defend
  against Parchi being sold — the keys go with it. That's a gap, and it's a
  one-way door, so resolve it alongside the open M1 key-recovery decision rather
  than separately.

  The strong version is architectural: split key custody so part of it sits
  outside the estate, and an acquirer receives ciphertext they cannot open.
  Second-order payoff is large — "if we die, your records die with us, and 
  here's the cryptography that makes that true" is a one-sentence fact no Indian
  health app can currently say.

  3. Claim assembly is the wedge, not search.

  "Organise your records" has no deadline and no payer. That's why every PHR
  before yours died of indifference. "Assemble the documents for a claim due
  Friday and name the missing one" has both, plus a rupee value.

  It also partially solves your second-biggest risk: a first upload that returns
  60% coverage feels broken. Claim assembly is the one feature that stays
  useful at low coverage — knowing the admission summary is missing is valuable
  even if most values didn't anchor.

  So the build order changes: upload → documents → claim assembly → split viewer
  → Ask Parchi last. Ask is the demo, not the wedge.

  Two things to stop

  The landing page. Revision six, for a visitor arriving from a channel that
  doesn't exist. Commit and deploy the folk.com work — it's finished for now —
  then leave it. The channel determines what the page has to say, and you
  haven't named a channel.

  Adding a third stack. Next + Python is correct (coordinates are non-negotiable
  and every tool that returns them is Python), but two stacks for one person is
  already a standing tax.

  What survives review

  The provenance invariant is a genuine architectural moat — a competitor can't
  add it in a sprint because it constrains the entire pipeline — and it's also
  your broadcastable fact. Paid-only revenue passes Munger's incentive test with
  exactly one customer. And the 42-point self-audit that caught your own
  provenance rule being circular is the strongest signal in the project; almost
  nobody audits their design before writing product code.

  Staying at one person is fine. Kailash Nadh runs 15–20% of India's daily
  trading volume with about 35 people.

  They all turned a limitation into a weapon. Zerodha had no money for ads, so
  they built something people would talk about — and even after they got rich,
  they still don't advertise. Zoho refused investors for 30 years. Levels runs a
  ₹2.5 crore/month business on ₹17,000/month of costs. The trick isn't being
  poor. It's picking a restriction and never letting it go.

  They think about failure first, not success. Munger's whole method is: figure
  out what would kill you, then don't go there. Much cheaper than trying to be
  brilliant.

  They keep things small on purpose. Zerodha's tech team is 35 people and runs a
  fifth of India's daily stock trades. They hired 5 people in 4 years. Nobody
  in this list treats "more people" as progress.

  to protect people's DNA. Then they went bankrupt, and 15 million people's
  genetic data got sold off — legally — because the promise was written in a
  privacy policy instead of built into the system. A policy dies when the
  company dies.

  Almost everyone gets distribution wrong. Thiel's point: most startups fail
  from nobody finding them, not from a bad product. You need one channel that
  works, not five you half-tried.

  ---

  What this means for parchi

  1. Your biggest risk isn't the tech. It's that nobody uploads anything.
  Less than 5% of Indians with smartphones have ever opened a health record
  digitally. You're building beautiful extraction accuracy for people who
  haven't shown up yet. Go watch one caregiver actually try it. You haven't
  talked to a single user.

  2. You need a "what if parchi dies" answer.
  Right now, if someone dumps your database they get gibberish — good. But if
  someone buys parchi, they get the keys too. That's exactly the 23andMe hole.

  your best line ever: "if we shut down, your records become unreadable, and

  ---

  Part 1 — Parchi

  Your two capabilities are not equal. One is the product, the other is a 
  liability.

  Capability 2 — the doctor summary — is the product, not a feature on top

  Indian super-specialty OPDs average 2.2 minutes per patient. Gastroenterology,
  neurology and oncology clinics see 200+ patients a day at roughly two minutes
  each. A caregiver arriving with 26 documents and seven years of history has
  no mechanism to transfer any of it.

  The folder was never the bottleneck. The two minutes is.

  It also passes every test the others fail:

  ┌─────────────────────┬───────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │                     │                                                   │
  ├─────────────────────┼───────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
  │ Deadline            │ The appointment                                   │
  ├─────────────────────┼───────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
  │ Payer               │ Avoided repeat tests, avoided a missed            │
  │                     │ interaction                                       │
  ├─────────────────────┼───────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
  │ Frequency           │ Every visit — recurring, not episodic             │
  ├─────────────────────┼───────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
  │ Can ChatGPT do it?  │ No. Needs the full multi-year corpus, which only  │
  │                     │ you hold                                          │
  ├─────────────────────┼───────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
  │ Does provenance     │ It's the whole thing. No doctor acts on a summary │
  │ matter?             │  they can't check                                 │
  └─────────────────────┴───────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

  That last row is the strategic unlock. Everywhere else in Parchi, provenance
  is a trust feature you have to explain. Here it's what makes the output usable
  at all.

  This reverses what I told you earlier. I ranked claim assembly as the wedge. I
  was wrong on frequency — claims happen once every few years, doctor visits
  recur every few months. Claim assembly stays, as the second wedge.

  Capability 1 — explaining reports — cut it back hard

  Three reasons, in order of severity:

  It has no moat. A user can photograph a report and ask ChatGPT right now. Your
  one durable asset — the anchored corpus — contributes nothing to explaining a
  single document.

  The line you're drawing doesn't survive a real user. "Explain, don't diagnose"
  is clean on paper. A person reading "haemoglobin 9.2, below the printed range
  of 13–17" has been told they're anaemic, whatever the disclaimer says. And
  this isn't theoretical: a 2026 BMJ Open audit found 49.6% of AI chatbot health
  answers were problematic, and separate research shows people over-trust AI 
  medical responses, rating them as valid as doctors' despite low accuracy.
  You'd be shipping the exact failure mode the whole product exists to avoid.

  Regulatory. CDSCO finalised its Medical Device Software guidance on 23 July 
  2026 — last month. Interpreting a patient's value against a range is precisely
  the boundary between "filing tool" and "regulated device."

  Build a grounded glossary instead. Tap a term: what the test measures, what
  the unit is, what range is printed on this page. Never a sentence about the
  user's number. Cheap, safe, honest about being a dictionary.

  The thing missing from both your framing and mine

  Parchi is defended against the wrong failure.

  Your provenance invariant defends against commission — inventing a value that
  isn't on the page. The research says that's the rare failure:

  - AI clinical summaries: omissions ~25%, inaccuracies ~20%, hallucinations ~2%
  - Physician review of 147 AI chart summaries: 46 omissions vs 5 hallucinations
  - Stated explicitly: errors of omission may represent a larger threat than 
    errors of commission
  - 21 of 109 unedited summaries rated as mild harm potential, one as moderate

  The dangerous summary isn't the one that invents a cardiac event. It's the one
  that omits the 2021 cardiac event, looks complete, and gets relied on by a
  doctor with two minutes.

  You cannot anchor an absence. Anchoring is structurally incapable of catching
  this.

  The fix, and it's the strongest idea here

  Every summary carries a machine-computed coverage block it cannot suppress:

  Built from 26 documents · Feb 2019 – Aug 2026
  No records held: Mar 2021 – Jan 2022
  No medication list after Jun 2025
  4 documents present but not machine-read (handwritten)
  Blood pressure: never recorded in any uploaded document

  Three payoffs at once. It makes the artefact safe to hand a doctor, because
  they can see the boundary of what they're being told. It turns your biggest
  liability into your most distinctive feature — nobody else does this. And a
  visible gap is a to-do list, which drives uploads better than any prompt.

  Framing that must never bend: "the documents I hold", never "your medical 
  history."

  The strongest version

  I derived these from where our own work actually failed, not from a taxonomy

  from a proxy signal, tag verified vs inferred, declare coverage on every
  summary, absence is a finding.

  identical in shape: the SVG grain layer that collapsed rendering from 500
  frames to 9 in 45 seconds; the 198MB export because grain is incompressible
  and I never checked against the delivery target; the mux that ran while the
  renderer was still writing the file. Rules: measure the expensive path once
  before committing, design to the destination constraint not the source, put
  work in the layer that does it natively, verify preconditions.

  (safety → hold, strategy → say once then execute, taste → defer fast), never
  raise the same concern twice, and "it's boring" is a defect report not a
  preference.

  to the other three and to your existing impeccable / design-taste-frontend

  What I actually think about the restructured ideation

  You found the product. I don't think you fully realise what you found.

  You framed the doctor summary as the second of two capabilities to add on top
  of the organiser. It isn't a capability. It's the reason the organiser exists,
  and everything else in Parchi should be reorganised beneath it.

  Here's what convinced me, and it's not the feature itself — it's what it does
  to the rest of your architecture:

  It retroactively justifies your hardest engineering decision. You built the
  provenance invariant on principle, before you had a user who needed it.
  Everywhere else in the product, anchoring is a trust feature you have to
  explain to people — nice, defensible, but slightly abstract. In front of a
  doctor with 2.2 minutes, it stops being a principle and becomes the only
  reason the output is usable at all. No clinician acts on an AI summary they
  can't check. You accidentally built the moat before you found the thing it 
  protects.

  That's rare and you should take it seriously. Most founders find the feature
  first and bolt on the defensibility later, badly.

  Where your framing is still wrong

  You're treating both capabilities as additive. They're not symmetric — one
  strengthens the position and one dissolves it. Report explanation isn't a
  smaller version of the summary, it's the opposite kind of product:
  single-document, no corpus, no moat, and it puts you on the wrong side of the
  line you drew yourself. Shipping both signals you haven't decided what Parchi
  is.

  "Explain what the report says" is not actually a smaller claim than 
  "diagnose." That's the part I'd push hardest on. You wrote the distinction
  confidently, and it doesn't survive contact with a tired caregiver at 11pm.
  The disclaimer doesn't do the work you need it to do.

  The thing neither of us had

  Your instinct — "ideally traceable back to document, page and section" — was
  right but incomplete. Traceability is a defence against invention. Your real 
  exposure is omission, and no amount of anchoring touches it, because you
  cannot anchor an absence.

  Once I found the numbers (25% omissions vs 2% hallucinations in AI clinical
  summaries), the whole safety story flipped. Your architecture is well-defended
  against the rare failure and undefended against the common one.

  The coverage block is the fix, and I'd go further than I did in the document:
  it should be the first thing on the page, not a footnote. A summary that opens
  with "here is what I don't have" is a fundamentally different object from one
  that opens with findings. It changes how a doctor reads everything below it.
  It's also, bluntly, the most differentiated thing in the entire product —
  every competitor's AI summary projects false completeness because admitting
  gaps looks like weakness. It isn't. In clinical context it's the only thing
  that makes the artefact usable.

  What worries me

  Compression, not extraction. Two minutes means twenty seconds of reading.
  Every instinct in this project so far has been toward thoroughness — the
  42-point audit, the provenance chain, the Devanagari handling. All good, all
  the opposite of the skill this feature needs. The summary is a ruthless 
  editing problem wearing an engineering costume, and it's the one thing your
  track record doesn't yet demonstrate.

  And the old risk hasn't moved. Still zero user interviews. The summary gives
  people a reason to upload, but it's a reason that only pays off after they've
  done the work. That ordering problem is unsolved, and it's still the likeliest
  way this dies.

  The honest verdict

  The restructured ideation is substantially stronger than the original, and the
  improvement isn't from adding intelligence — it's from finally naming a
  moment. "Organise your records" had no moment. "You're seeing a new doctor on
  Thursday" does.

  Cut capability one to a glossary. Build the coverage engine before the
  summary. And before writing any of it, hand-make one summary from a real
  folder and put it in front of a doctor with a stopwatch. If they can't use it
  in twenty seconds, the format is wrong, and no amount of extraction accuracy
  will save it.
